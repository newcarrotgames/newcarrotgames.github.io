Reading it

The amber curve drops past about 5 kpc because most of the visible mass sits inside that radius. Farther out, a star feels roughly the pull of a single central mass, so its speed falls off like 1/√r. Planets around the Sun behave the same way.

The white circles are measured speeds of real Milky Way stars, from the Gaia and APOGEE surveys. The violet curve follows them: about 231 km/s at the Sun, falling slowly to about 180 km/s at 30 kpc. With Newton’s law, that only works if the enclosed mass keeps growing well past where the starlight fades. That extra, unseen mass is the halo, about 180 billion Suns’ worth, roughly three times the visible mass. Its shape and size here are the best fit to those measurements, from Ou and colleagues (2024).

The teal curve uses only the visible mass, but changes the law. Once gravity’s pull drops below a₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s², MOND says it weakens more slowly than Newton predicts. In the Milky Way that happens around 8 kpc, close to the Sun. The halo was fitted to the measurements. MOND’s curve wasn’t: it follows from the visible mass and a₀ alone. It runs 5 to 8% slower than the measured speeds between 6 and 20 kpc, about 15 km/s slow at the Sun. Estimates of the Milky Way’s visible mass vary by enough that this gap doesn’t settle the question.

The five white stars started in a straight line. Each trail shows how far that star moved in the last 100 million years. The second one sits about where the Sun does, 8 kpc from the center.

With Stars pull on each other turned on, each disk also feels its own gravity, and within a few hundred million years each one forms a central bar. The Newton-only disk can’t keep its outer stars in place: the bar pushes them outward, and some escape even though they started at balanced speeds. The halo’s extra mass keeps its disk calm. That was one of the earliest arguments for dark matter (Ostriker and Peebles, 1973). The MOND disk forms a bar too, and it spreads out more than the halo galaxy does.

Simplified model of the Milky Way. Visible matter follows de Salas and colleagues (2019): a 15.5-billion-Sun bulge (Hernquist profile), a 36.5-billion-Sun stellar disk with a 2.35 kpc scale length, and 9.5 billion Suns of atomic and molecular gas in thin exponential disks. The dark matter halo is the Einasto best fit of Ou and colleagues (2024, MNRAS 528, 693), whose circular speeds from about 120,000 stars are plotted. The bulge, gas and halo are smooth and fixed. With “Stars pull on each other” on, each of the 5,000 or so disk stars carries an equal share of the stellar disk’s mass and pulls on all the others. That pull is computed in the disk’s plane on a 128 × 128 grid, using an FFT (a particle-mesh solver). The disk starts with enough random motion that it doesn’t break up into clumps (Toomre Q = 1.5). With it off, every star moves in the fixed, averaged pull of the starting disk, and the arms wind up over time. Real arms don’t wind up, because they are density waves, not fixed groups of stars. MOND uses the “simple” interpolating function, applied to the combined pull of all visible matter. A full MOND calculation solves a modified field equation instead. In the real Milky Way the bar makes motions inside about 6 kpc non-circular, and the halo responds to the disk. The outer measurements are still debated: some Gaia studies find a steeper decline past 20 kpc, while gas tracers suggest a flatter curve. Rotation curves are where MOND does best. One of its harder tests, a collision between galaxy clusters, is below.

The Bullet Cluster

Rotation curves can be explained with dark matter or with MOND. A head-on crash between two galaxy clusters is much harder to explain without dark matter, because it pulls the ordinary matter and the mass apart.

Three parts, three behaviors

Galaxies are small and far apart, so the two sets pass through each other with almost no collisions.

Hot gas fills the space between the galaxies and holds most of the ordinary matter, several times more than the galaxies themselves. The two gas clouds hit each other, slow down and get left behind in the middle. The bright arc ahead of the bullet’s gas is a shock front, like a sonic boom.

Dark matter, if it barely interacts with anything, should pass straight through along with the galaxies.

What was measured

Chandra’s X-ray images show where the gas is. Gravitational lensing shows where the mass is: the cluster’s gravity slightly stretches the images of galaxies behind it, and mapping that stretch maps the mass. In 2006, Douglas Clowe and colleagues found the mass peaks sitting on the galaxies, well away from the gas, at 8σ significance.

If the gas made up most of the mass, the lensing peaks would sit on the pink clouds. Switch to “If there were no dark matter” to see that version. It isn’t what was observed.

It does show that most of this system’s mass is not in the gas.
